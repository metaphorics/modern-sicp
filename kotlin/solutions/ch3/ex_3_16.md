## Exercise 3.16: Ben's count-pairs and its three wrong answers

Approach: `E3_16.kt` is Ben's recursion verbatim in Kotlin: a non-pair
counts 0, a pair counts `car` + `cdr` + 1. The solution's job is not to
fix it but to exhibit the structures that expose it, so
`E3_16Test.kt` builds the three canonical three-pair structures and
asserts Ben's answers.

The three structures: the chain `(a b c)` has three pairs reachable by
exactly one path each, so Ben's count is right: 3. The second structure,
`cons(x, x.cdr)` with `x = (a b)`, has three physical pairs, but the
`x` pair is reachable both as the car and -- through its own `cdr` --
as the cdr of the top pair, so it is counted on two paths: 4. The third,
`cons(p, p)` with `p = cons(q, q)` and `q = (a)`, sends every path
through both arms at every level: 1 + 3 + 3 = 7, again with three
physical pairs. For the fourth structure any cycle -- for instance the
`makeCycle` ring of exercise 3.13 -- walks forever, because the
recursion only stops at `VNil` and the ring never reaches one; the test
suite asserts the finite cases and leaves the divergent one unbuilt.

Why the counts are wrong: the procedure counts *visits*, not *pairs*.
Without remembering what it has seen, a shared pair is indistinguishable
from two equal-looking pairs, and the count grows with the number of
paths through the structure. Exercise 3.17 fixes exactly this.

What the tests say: three structures, three asserted counts (3, 4, 7).
A "corrected" `countPairs` fails the 4 and 7 rows; a Ben's version with
an off-by-one fails all of them.

Class: T (direct port; the structures are the book's own answer).

Deviation: none.
