# Exercise 1.22: a timed search for primes over odd integers

## Approach

`timedPrimeTest` keeps the statement's shape with `performance.now()` in
place of Scheme's `runtime`: the clock runs around `isPrime`, and the
result is a record `{ n, elapsedMillis }` instead of a printed line, so
the search's data can be asserted. `searchForPrimes` walks consecutive
odd integers above `start` until it holds three primes.

## Numbers

The three smallest primes above 1000 are `[1009, 1013, 1019]`, above
10000 `[10007, 10009, 10037]`, above 100000 `[100003, 100019, 100043]`,
above 1000000 `[1000003, 1000033, 1000037]` - the same twelve the
sibling editions pin. `timedPrimeTest(100)` is `null`;
`timedPrimeTest(101)` reports a finite, nonnegative elapsed time. The
raw timings themselves are not asserted, on purpose: on this host a
prime test near 1000 costs single-digit microseconds, below the ~100
microsecond coarsening of `performance.now()` without cross-origin
isolation, and the JIT warms up while the search runs, so first-run
readings are noise. The Theta(sqrt(n)) shape only becomes measurable in
the median harness of exercise 1.22a, where the measured medians over
the four thresholds grow roughly as the square roots, minus loop
overheads and JIT flat spots the statement asks about.

## Class and deviations

Adapted row, per the map: "performance.now replaces runtime". The
book's display-and-three-asterisks protocol is replaced by a returned
record (the interaction convention's data-over-display rule); the
deterministic assertions are the pinned primes and the timing record's
shape, never a wall-clock threshold.
