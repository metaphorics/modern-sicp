# Exercise 4.7: let* as nested lets

## Approach

`letStarToNestedLets` peels off the outermost binding as a one-binding
let around the rest: `(let* ((v1 e1) (v2 e2) ...) body)` becomes
`(let ((v1 e1)) (let* ((v2 e2) ...) body))`, and with no bindings left
only the body remains. The dispatch installs both let and let*, since the
expansion produces plain let forms; this answers the book's closing
question: adding the clause `(eval (let*->nested-lets exp) env)` is
enough once let is a clause of the same evaluator, and no non-derived
expressions are needed.

## Numbers

The book's example `(let* ((x 3) (y (+ x 2)) (z (+ x y 5))) (* x z))` is
`39`. The rewrite of `(let* ((x 3) (y (+ x 2))) (* x y))` prints exactly
as `(let ((x 3)) (let ((y (+ x 2))) (* x y)))`. Each initializer sees the
previous bindings: `(let* ((x 3) (y (+ x 1))) (+ x y))` is `7`. Empty
bindings: `(let* () 42)` is `42`. In a body: `(g)` with
`(define (g) (let* ((a 2) (b (* a 3))) (+ a b)))` is `8`.

## Class and deviations

Translated row. Deviation: the dispatch carries a let clause alongside
the let* clause, because the expansion terminates in plain lets; the
book's question is answered in the affirmative by construction.
