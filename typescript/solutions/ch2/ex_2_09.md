# Exercise 2.9: the width of a sum is a function of the widths; the width of a product is not

## Approach

`addWidthLaw` checks the additive law exactly: `[2, 6] + [10, 14]` has
width 4.0, the sum of the two widths 2.0 + 2.0, because the sum's bounds
are the addends' bounds added pairwise, so half their difference is the sum
of the halves; the difference behaves the same with the bounds crossed.
For multiplication the claim fails: `[2, 6] * [10, 14]` and
`[2, 6] * [100, 104]` use factors of identical widths (2.0 and 2.0), yet
the products' widths are 32.0 and 212.0 - the width of a product depends on
the factors' centers too. The same two equal-width divisors give quotient
widths of about 0.2286 and 0.0204.

The map's `A` decision is stated in the module doc comment and enforced by
the section's constructor: an interval exists only through
`makeInterval`, which requires `lo <= hi` and reports `UnorderedBounds`
otherwise, so `width` is always well defined and every call site inherits
the invariant instead of re-deciding it.

## Numbers

Additive law: 4 = 2 + 2, exactly, in both the sum and the difference.
Counterexamples: product widths 32.0 vs 212.0 under equal argument widths;
quotient widths 0.2285714285714286 vs 0.020384615384615383.

## Map class

`A` (map: "width forces interval invariant decision"): the statement
survives, and implementing it forced the once-only decision this edition
makes in `makeInterval` - `lo <= hi`, reported, never silently swapped.

## Deviation

None.
