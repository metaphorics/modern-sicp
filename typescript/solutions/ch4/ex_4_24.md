# Exercise 4.24: benchmark analysis versus execution

## Approach

One recursive arithmetic workload, `fib(14)` over a recursive `fib`,
runs under the engine's direct `evaluate` and under
`analyze(...)(...)`, each over its own environment built from the same
admitted setup. After two untimed warmups, seven calls are timed with
`performance.now()`; each path reports the median of its seven runs,
the raw runs are returned alongside, and the analysis fraction is
`(direct - analyzed) / direct` — the share of direct time attributable
to re-analyzing syntax.

## Numbers

The prior recorded observation on the old engine: direct runs `54.0,
46.3, 45.7, 46.1, 47.4, 50.1, 50.6` ms, median `47.4` ms; analyzed runs
`74.6, 70.2, 51.6, 45.8, 44.7, 44.3, 43.1` ms, median `45.8` ms;
fraction `3%`. Two further pairs gave `+6%` (48.6 vs 45.7 ms) and
`-12%` (46.2 vs 51.5 ms). The analysis share for this workload is
small, at most around ten percent, and the sign is not stable across
runs because individual runs spread about as wide as the medians'
difference. Those timings are historical evidence from the old
per-step engine; the migrated engine's numbers are parent-executed
observations and are not claimed here — the test pins the protocol
(seven runs per path, positive medians, the fraction formula), not
machine-specific milliseconds.

## Class and deviations

Translated row. Deviations: the workload is `fib` rather than one of
the book's suggested procedures, chosen for a call-heavy profile;
medians of seven timed runs after two warmups; and the fraction is
reported with its noise, since on this host the difference between the
paths is within run-to-run variance. Unlike the book's Lisp, where
`eval`'s dispatch is a large share of each step, this engine's per-step
work dominates both paths equally, so separating analysis from
execution buys little here.
