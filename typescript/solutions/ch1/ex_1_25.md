# Exercise 1.25: Alyssa's expmod under f64 precision and bigint cost

## Approach

She is right about the mathematics: `remainder(fast-expt base exp) m`
equals `expmod base exp m` over the integers. She is wrong about the
machine, twice over on this host. First, `number` is an IEEE double:
`fastExpt(3, 40)` is already past `2^53`, so its low bits are gone and
the final `%` answers a rounded question - `3^40 mod 101` is truly `87`,
the number version computes `54`. Past about `10^308` the product is
`Infinity`, and `Infinity % m` is `NaN`, which is what the prime tester
would see at exponents near a million. Second, the honest fix -
`fastExptBigint` - is exact, but it multiplies numbers the size of the
full exponential (47,000+ digits at exponent 100,003), while the
section's `expmod` keeps every intermediate below `m^2` and never leaves
machine-word territory. Exact-but-slow versus bounded-and-fast is the
tradeoff the statement asks to state.

## Numbers

`expmod(3, 40, 101)` is `87`; `expmodSimplified(3, 40, 101)` is not
`87` (precision already gone). `expmod(3, 1000003, 1000003)` is `3`;
`expmodSimplified` there is `NaN`. The bigint restatement agrees with
`expmod` everywhere it was run: `expmodSimplifiedBigint(3n, 100003,
1000003n)` is `575610n`, matching the number `expmod`'s answer
`575610`, and `expmodSimplifiedBigint(2n, 1015, 561n)` is `230n`.

## Class and deviations

Adapted row, per the map: "f64 precision, bigint slow". The statement
gained the two-layer host analysis (precision loss at `2^53`,
`Infinity`/`NaN` past `10^308`, bigint exactness at digit cost) and the
solutions pin both layers. Base 2 is deliberately avoided in the
precision demo - powers of two are exactly representable doubles - so
the test uses base 3, where the rounding bites.
