# Exercise 4.22: let in the analyzed evaluator

## Approach

A let is a derived expression, so `analyze-let` analyzes the combination it
stands for: `(let ((x e) ...) body...)` becomes the analysis of
`((lambda (x ...) body...) e ...)`. The analyzer is written out locally
with the let clause added, because the module's analyze helpers recurse
through the module's `analyze` and a let nested in a lambda, if, or
application body would otherwise die as `UnknownSyntax`. With the clause,
closures made by the analyzed lambda store their body's execution
procedure, and `evalAnalyzed`-style evaluation runs lets with no let
machinery at run time.

## Numbers

`(let ((x 3)) (+ x 4))` through the analyzed evaluator answers `7`.
`(define (f y) (let ((x (* y y))) (+ x y)))` then `(f 4)` answers `20`,
the nested-body case. Direct evaluation of the fully derived form
`((lambda (x) ((lambda (y) (* x y)) (+ x 1))) 3)` answers `12`, and the
analyzed evaluator answers `12` for `(let ((x 3)) (let ((y (+ x 1))) (* x y)))`:
the derivation agrees with hand-written combinations. Running the execution
procedure returned by `analyzeLet` on a fresh global environment answers `7`.

## Class and deviations

Translated row. Deviation: the module's `analyzeSequence` and
`analyzeApplication` are mirrored locally rather than reused, for the
nested-body reason above; leaf analyzers (`analyze-self-evaluating`,
`analyze-quoted`, `analyze-variable`) and `execute-application` are
reused from the module unchanged.
